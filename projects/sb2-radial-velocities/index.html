Data Analysis

Radial Velocities of a Double-Lined Spectroscopic Binary

I measured how fast each of the two stars in a binary system is moving, using Bayesian statistics and a Monte Carlo sampler.

Year
2025
Context
Data science course assignment, University of Geneva
Method
Gaussian model fitting, MCMC
Built with
Python, NumPy, SciPy

The physics

The star HD 195521 is actually two stars orbiting each other. We can't see them separately, but their light is recorded together. As they orbit, one moves towards us while the other moves away, and the Doppler effect shifts the light of each star by a different amount.

In the data, each star shows up as a dip. The position of the dip gives that star's velocity along our line of sight, its radial velocity. Observed on three different nights, the two dips move apart, merge, and separate again on the other side.

What I did

  • Modelled each observation as two Gaussian dips, one per star, and made a first estimate of their positions.
  • Wrote the log-likelihood of the data given the model, with an extra noise term because the quoted errors were underestimated.
  • Combined it with priors to get the posterior probability of the parameters.
  • Sampled the posterior with MCMC, a Monte Carlo method, to get each star's velocity and its uncertainty.
  • Fitted the three observations together, sharing the shape of each star's dip across nights, which helps most for the overlapping one.

Learning the statistics

The fitting itself was standard data analysis. What made this project useful for me was learning the statistics behind it, such as likelihoods, priors, posteriors and MCMC, and applying them to a real problem instead of a textbook example.

The sampler takes a random walk through the parameters. At first it wanders, then it settles. That first stretch, the burn-in, is thrown away, and the rest gives the result and its uncertainty.

What I learned

  • Writing a likelihood and priors for a real model
  • Running MCMC and checking it: convergence, burn-in, uncertainties
  • Reading parameter correlations from corner plots
  • Fitting models to real astronomical data
